# Social Proof

## What
Social Proof uses credible evidence from other users, customers, or behaviour to reduce uncertainty.

People use others' actions and outcomes as decision signals.

## Why it matters
Users hesitate when they are unsure whether a product is real, trusted, or effective.

Good social proof:
- builds confidence
- reduces perceived risk
- validates the promise
- supports conversion decisions
- makes claims more believable

Proof must be specific and relevant.

## When to apply
- landing pages
- pricing pages
- signup flows
- checkout
- feature pages
- enterprise or trust-heavy offers

## How to apply

- place proof near the claim it supports
- use specific customer outcomes where possible
- show recognizable logos only when relevant
- use testimonials that describe a real before and after
- avoid inflated or unverifiable claims
- match proof to the user's stage of decision

The proof should answer the user's doubt at that moment.

## Implementation rules

- proof must support a specific claim
- testimonials must be concrete, not generic praise
- metrics must be believable and contextual
- logos must not replace explanation
- proof should not interrupt the primary path
- avoid fake urgency or fabricated popularity

## Example

Bad:
- "Loved by everyone"
- anonymous generic testimonial
- logo wall with no context
- proof far from the claim

Good:
- named customer quote
- specific result or use case
- proof beside the relevant CTA
- metrics with clear context

## Fail conditions

- proof feels fake
- proof is too vague to reduce doubt
- proof is irrelevant to the audience
- proof distracts from action
- proof claims cannot be trusted

## Enforcement rule

If proof does not make the promise more believable, replace it with specific, relevant evidence or remove it.
