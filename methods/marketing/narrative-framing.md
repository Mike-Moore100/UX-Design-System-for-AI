# Narrative Framing

## What
Narrative Framing shapes the story around a product: what changed, why old solutions are failing, and why this product is the logical response.

The narrative should make the product feel necessary, not merely available.

## Why it matters
Users remember stories more easily than disconnected claims.

Strong narrative framing:
- creates urgency
- clarifies stakes
- improves memorability
- connects features to meaning
- supports brand authority

Without a narrative, messaging becomes a list.

## When to apply
- homepages
- launch pages
- sales decks
- product announcements
- thought leadership
- category pages
- founder-led messaging

## How to apply

- identify the change in the world or market
- name the pain created by that change
- explain why existing approaches no longer work
- introduce the product as the response
- connect proof to the narrative
- end with a concrete action

The user should understand why this solution matters now.

## Implementation rules

- narrative must be rooted in a real user or market shift
- stakes must be specific
- product claims must connect to the narrative
- avoid dramatic language without evidence
- do not let story obscure the actual product
- CTA must match the narrative's next step

## Example

Bad:
- starts with brand origin story only
- uses broad claims about the future
- lists features without context
- ends without a clear action

Good:
- clear market shift
- specific user pain
- product presented as the natural answer
- proof reinforces the story

## Fail conditions

- story is generic
- user problem is unclear
- product appears late or vaguely
- claims are not supported
- narrative does not lead to action

## Enforcement rule

If the story does not explain why the product should exist now, sharpen the narrative before adding more claims.
