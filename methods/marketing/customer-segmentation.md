# Customer Segmentation

## What
Customer Segmentation groups users by meaningful differences in needs, intent, maturity, value, or behaviour.

Segments should guide product and messaging decisions.

## Why it matters
Different customers buy for different reasons.

Strong segmentation:
- improves relevance
- clarifies offers
- supports pricing
- sharpens onboarding
- prevents generic positioning

Bad segmentation creates complexity without insight.

## When to apply
- positioning
- onboarding
- pricing
- lifecycle messaging
- landing pages
- sales qualification
- product strategy

## How to apply

- segment by behaviour, jobs, maturity, or value where possible
- validate that segments have different needs
- define the best message, proof, and CTA for each segment
- avoid segments that cannot be acted on
- keep the number of segments manageable
- revisit segments as the product matures

Each segment should change a real decision.

## Implementation rules

- segments must be distinct and actionable
- segmentation must connect to user needs or business value
- messaging must adapt meaningfully by segment
- segments must not rely on stereotypes
- data collection must be proportionate and respectful
- default experience must still work for unknown users

## Example

Bad:
- segments by age only with no behaviour difference
- too many personas no one uses
- messaging barely changes by segment
- asks intrusive questions to personalise weakly

Good:
- segments by use case or readiness
- different proof and CTA by segment
- onboarding adapts to maturity
- sales routing matches account value

## Fail conditions

- segment does not change decisions
- groups are too broad or too many
- assumptions are unvalidated
- personalisation becomes intrusive
- default experience is neglected

## Enforcement rule

If a segment does not change message, offer, flow, or prioritisation, remove or redefine it.
