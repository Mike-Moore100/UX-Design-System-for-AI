# Positioning

## What
Positioning defines where the product sits in the user's mind: who it is for, what category it belongs to, and why it is different.

Positioning makes the product easier to understand and remember.

## Why it matters
Users compare products against alternatives, not in isolation.

Strong positioning:
- clarifies the audience
- reduces category confusion
- strengthens differentiation
- improves messaging
- makes the offer easier to evaluate

If positioning is unclear, everything downstream becomes vague.

## When to apply
- homepages
- landing pages
- product launches
- feature pages
- comparison pages
- pricing pages
- onboarding intros

## How to apply

- define the target user
- name the category or alternative
- state the main problem solved
- explain the unique advantage
- connect the promise to a concrete outcome
- remove claims that could describe any competitor

The user should know why this product exists and why it is different.

## Implementation rules

- headline and intro must signal category or use case
- audience must be specific enough to feel relevant
- differentiation must be concrete
- avoid generic claims like faster, better, easier without proof
- message must match product reality
- comparison should be clear without attacking competitors unnecessarily

## Example

Bad:
- "A better way to work"
- unclear audience
- no category signal
- generic benefits

Good:
- clear audience
- recognizable category
- specific difference
- outcome tied to user pain

## Fail conditions

- user cannot tell what the product is
- message could apply to any product
- audience is too broad
- differentiation is vague
- positioning conflicts with the actual product

## Enforcement rule

If the product cannot be described clearly against an alternative, sharpen the positioning before writing more copy.
